What does the evidence establish?

The case for protection and the limits of what can be concluded belong together.

Design dimensions

Height depends on the measurement point

The April 2018 task-force recommendation specified a minimum of 7 feet 6 inches above the highest toehold. The City FAQ, checked September 13, 2026, describes 8–11 feet above the nearest toehold and says its presented options use 10 feet. These statements use different wording and dates. They do not, by themselves, establish a formally adopted replacement standard. Neither measurement should be confused with total height above the sidewalk.

Public Safety Committee · April 18, 2018 · Presentation · PDF p. 34 ↗ City height FAQ ↗

Prevention rationale

A barrier can create time

The research describes how interrupting access can allow an immediate crisis to ease or create an opportunity for intervention. The Clifton Suspension Bridge study reported fewer deaths and staff accounts of more time to intervene, even without fewer incidents.

Clifton also used cameras and patrols. That study does not isolate extra time as the sole mechanism, and crisis-duration research does not establish one timeline for every person.

Bennewith, Nowers & Gunnell · 2011 · European Journal of Public Health ↗2024 presentation · p. 43 ↗
Substitution

Another location is not an inevitable outcome

The research summarized here challenges the assumption that restricting one site simply moves every death elsewhere. Longer follow-up can also change an initial finding, as it did at Toronto’s Bloor Viaduct.

The 2025 meta-analysis found no clear increase at other sites and fewer deaths using the same method overall. It did not detect a reduction across all suicide methods combined, and some comparison-site data were limited.

Local design choice

Evidence for barriers is not a universal ranking of designs

The Swiss study cited in the early City presentations grouped complete vertical barriers and nets together. It did not establish that one type was more effective than the other.

Pasadena’s preference also reflected its own assessment of architecture, engineering, and emergency-services concerns. The 2024 netting assessment still had unfinished engineering work.

2024 survey

Respondents expressed doubts and design preferences

The survey drew 678 responses overall. On whether a vertical barrier would help prevent suicides, the City reported 40% yes, 33% no, and 27% don’t know. That overall response count is not an established denominator for every question. Some respondents skipped the design ranking.

Metal pickets ranked highest as the preferred material and design. The City reported 81% support for added lighting and unanimous Historic Preservation Commission support for lighting under the balustrade.

Public Safety Committee · July 17, 2024 · Survey summary · PDF p. 18 ↗ · Commission feedback · PDF p. 16 ↗

Participation was self-selected. The results are not a representative poll of Pasadena residents. The 2021 comment coding and this survey’s direct questions are different measures, not a trend.

READING THE MONEY AND DATES

The design-phase account paid for more than design fees

$1.48 millionLife-to-date spending recorded against project 73324 through June 30, 2026, rounded from $1,475,508.
June 2028The reported design-phase target, not a promised opening date for a completed barrier.

The FY2026 year-end row listed $2,874,000 appropriated to project 73324, titled “Colorado Street Bridge Barrier Enhancements – Design Phase.” The August 2021 report describes earlier project spending on temporary fencing and maintenance, mockups, environmental assessment, conceptual design, and outreach. The later total should not be read as a total of architects’ fees. The row does not break down spending by those uses.

The 2021 report estimated $4–6 million to complete design and construction. In April 2026, staff separately estimated construction at roughly $4 million. Those estimates have different scopes. They do not establish a cost decrease.

The project’s appropriations reached $3,050,000: $950,000 from the General Fund, $100,000 in Gas Tax funding, and $2 million labeled ARPA. In 2025, the Council transferred $130,000 on June 9 and $46,000 on July 21 to the San Rafael Bridge Seismic Retrofit project, reducing the total to $2,874,000. Staff said enough remained to finish design without affecting its progress. Construction still required additional funding.

The annual capital budgets and approved Council actions reconcile the net appropriation to $2,874,000. They didn’t establish the available balance after outstanding commitments or the bridge-specific federal ARPA accounting.

2021 survey

Option B led among respondents who ranked the mockups

The City reported 2,268 responses overall. Of the 1,038 respondents who ranked the three designs, 216 chose Option A (20.8%), 462 chose Option B (44.5%), and 360 chose Option C (34.7%). Separately, staff coded 622 comments and reported that 44% preferred no barriers and a return to the bridge’s original state.

Option B also matched the Historic Preservation Commission’s April 20, 2021 recommendation, with modifications. The minutes record eight votes in favor, none against, and one member absent. This was an advisory recommendation, not Council approval of a permanent design.

Participation was self-selected, and these figures do not represent Pasadena residents as a whole. The ranking results and coded comments have different denominators. They should not be combined with the later survey as a trend.
