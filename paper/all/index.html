The main research review covers material through September 1, 2026. Later checks and additions are dated where they appear.

Section 1 of 13 · Open chapter

The bridge and its history

Open image

Figure 1. The bridge after its 1993 restoration, showing its curve and arches across the Arroyo Seco. BCA Associates.

The Colorado Street Bridge has carried two reputations for generations. It’s one of Pasadena’s great landmarks, a 1913 concrete span over the Arroyo Seco that has become part of the city’s identity. It’s also known as “Suicide Bridge.”2,35

The bridge is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, documented by the Historic American Engineering Record, and designated a Pasadena Cultural Heritage Landmark. Pasadena’s Bridge Party, a tradition dating to 1978, celebrates the bridge and the efforts to preserve it.1,37

Its big-screen history reaches back to Charlie Chaplin’s The Kid (1921), which used the bridge for a scene in which Edna Purviance’s character considers suicide.38 Nearly a century later, it appeared in La La Land (2016).39 The bridge has been a setting for stories, a gathering place, and a landmark people have worked to preserve. But its other history never went away.

Section 2 of 13 · Open chapter

2017–2018. An emergency response to an old problem

By 2017, that other history had become impossible to ignore. The City counted 28 deaths by suicide at the bridge from 2006 through 2016, and staff reported an increase in suicides and attempts. On July 19, staff gave the Public Safety Committee a presentation on deaths at the bridge and possible ways to prevent them.2,3

The presentation also put those deaths in a longer context. It reported more than 150 deaths by suicide at the bridge since 1919, including an estimated 79 during the 1930s. It showed fencing and netting designs the City had drawn up around 1932 and said a police detail at the bridge during that decade cost $20,000 a year.2

Even then, a barrier did not come quickly. A Glendale News-Press account said Pasadena moved slowly amid concerns about how safety measures would affect the bridge’s appearance, and the City did not install a 7½-foot mesh fence topped with barbed wire until 1937. Writing in 1992, the Los Angeles Times reported no deaths by suicide from the bridge during the two years after the fence was installed. Deaths later resumed, but the newspaper said no single year had again reached the levels recorded during the 1930s.35

That fence was later replaced. By 1956, Pasadena had installed an iron barrier that curved toward the sidewalk, and that work removed the bridge’s original concrete railing and seating alcoves. In 1989, the City was planning an earthquake retrofit of the bridge. The Los Angeles Times reported that the project would include reworking the iron barrier to reduce what it described as a “tunnel effect.” The City completed the retrofit in 1993, rebuilding the historic concrete railing and seating alcoves and installing wrought-iron fencing above the railing.2,36

That was the bridge the committee was considering in 2017. Staff said the rebuilt concrete railing and safety barrier together stood nearly eight feet high. But the way they fit together created footholds that reduced the effective barrier height to about four feet. Staff also identified all 20 restored alcoves and both ends of the bridge as places where people could climb over or get around the barrier.2,5,36

The presentation explained why closing those gaps could save lives. It addressed questions that would keep returning in later meetings about whether a barrier could prevent a death or simply move it elsewhere. Interrupting an attempt, it explained, could give an immediate suicidal crisis time to ease. Someone stopped at one bridge would not necessarily die by suicide elsewhere.2,7,14,19

According to the official minutes of the July 19 meeting, Mayor Terry Tornek supported emergency fencing at the bridge’s alcoves, recommended continuing it along the bridge’s ends and approaches, and asked staff to work with local architects and engineers on a response that would be effective, cost-effective, and aesthetically pleasing. Vice Mayor John J. Kennedy, who chaired the committee, supported the alcove fencing as an emergency measure and said it would give the City time to find a more permanent solution. Councilmember Tyron Hampton supported planting trees below the bridge, as several public speakers had suggested, along with fencing at its ends and approaches, cameras, and patrols.2

Pasadena’s response began with those restored alcoves, 20 semicircular seating areas that extended from the walkway and offered places to pause and look out over the Arroyo.2,36 The City fenced them first in 2017.4,5

Later in 2017, Public Works Director Ara Maloyan brought together a task force to study what the City should do next. The group included the City’s health officer and public health director, a suicide-prevention specialist from Didi Hirsch Mental Health Services, Police, Fire, architects, engineers, and preservation advocates.5,9

The task force considered physical barriers, netting, patrols, cameras, motion sensors, crisis phones, signs, lighting, and changes to landscaping and railings. One suggestion was to plant hedges below the bridge to spell “HOPE.” Some ideas were meant to keep a person from reaching the edge. Others were meant to make an intervention more likely.5,7

Some of those broad choices were not new. More than 80 years earlier, Pasadena had considered fencing, netting, and a police presence, and the bridge’s appearance had been a concern then as well.2,35 This time, the City moved quickly to put temporary fencing around the alcoves, but choosing a permanent barrier design would take much longer.2,4,28

Over Labor Day weekend in 2018, first responders spent 13 hours helping a person return to safety. Afterward, the City Manager issued an emergency purchase order for temporary fencing 10 feet high along the bridge’s full length.4,28

Open image

Figure 5. Temporary chain-link fencing along both sides of the bridge, as shown in the City’s November 2019 presentation. City of Pasadena.

A Police Department table presented to the committee in 2021 recorded 10 deaths in 2017, before the full-length fence, and four in 2018, the year it was installed. In the years that followed, it recorded one in 2019, none in 2020, and one through June 13, 2021. Pasadena Now quoted then-City Manager Steve Mermell in September 2018 as saying that nine people had died at the bridge in 2017. The difference remains unexplained. The available records do not provide a complete year-by-year count for recent years.3

A barrier can save a life by creating time. An immediate suicidal crisis can be brief, even when a person’s distress has lasted much longer. A barrier can delay or interrupt an attempt, giving the immediate crisis time to ease and first responders time to arrive. A physical barrier begins providing protection before anyone has noticed the crisis or arrived to help.5,19,29

The full-length fence changed the bridge’s appearance and restricted access. Lower death counts followed its installation. Its possible benefit was harder to see because deaths it may have prevented do not appear in incident totals. The record that followed would repeatedly separate the safety question from the design question. Years later, the emergency measure remains in place, and the City still calls it temporary, though keeping it is itself a choice.3,4,28

Section 3 of 13 · Open chapter

2018–2019. Pasadena chooses a policy direction

Months before the full-length fence went up, Councilmember Steve Madison supported moving forward with the task-force recommendation and asked staff to provide dimensions or a visual of the proposed barrier before the matter reached the full Council, according to the official minutes of the April 18, 2018 Public Safety Committee meeting. Tornek specifically requested a visual showing the scale of the proposed barriers. The task force’s written recommendation set a minimum barrier height of 7 feet 6 inches above the highest foothold, not simply above the walkway. The committee unanimously forwarded the recommendation to the Council.6

On April 23, Public Works presented the task force’s recommendation to the full City Council. Staff reviewed the alternatives the group had considered, including patrols, technology, netting, landscaping, and vertical barriers.7

Councilmember Hampton asked staff to consider temporary fencing at the bridge’s ends and approaches until a fence based on an approved design could be constructed.6

Councilmember Andy Wilson asked whether restricting access at the Colorado Street Bridge would simply push people to another location. Public Health Director Michael Johnson said research on barriers at bridges known for suicide suggested that they can reduce overall deaths rather than simply shift them elsewhere. Councilmember Victor Gordo followed up, asking whether a barrier would reduce suicides across the region or merely shift where they occurred. Johnson said the research showed an overall decline after barriers were installed and referred to a Toronto study.7

The discussion also briefly turned to the bridge’s transportation role. Councilmember Gene Masuda asked whether pedestrians could simply be prohibited. Maloyan said banning pedestrians from the bridge wouldn’t be enough. Preventing access would also require closing the bridge to vehicle traffic, which he called impossible.7

When Councilmember Margaret McAustin asked what the design consultant would actually study, Maloyan said the next phase would focus specifically on vertical barriers. The consultant would develop different versions of a barrier and bring them through review by preservation experts, designers, and the public.7

Tornek predicted that the night’s agreement was probably the highest level of consensus the process would reach.7

At the end of the discussion, Madison moved the staff recommendation, Hampton seconded it, and the Council unanimously adopted the task force’s direction toward a permanent physical deterrent.6 The task force’s recommendation centered on a physical barrier designed to keep people from reaching the edge. Other measures could still play a role, but the barrier became the foundation of the City’s approach.5,6

The Council had agreed on a barrier, but its design was still unsettled. In May 2018, it set aside $400,000 for environmental and design work. Public Works began seeking a consultant that October and interviewed two firms in February 2019.8

When the proposed contract reached the Public Safety Committee on April 17, 2019, Tornek objected that the schedule was too slow given the urgency of the issue, according to the official minutes. Madison cautioned that rushing projects had previously produced community and Council opposition, especially when a historic landmark was involved. He nevertheless supported a revised timeline and asked staff to review the proposed budget and pursue grant opportunities.8

When the committee returned to the contract on May 15, the official minutes record that Madison moved the revised recommendation, Tornek seconded it, and the committee approved it unanimously. At its May 20 meeting, the full Council approved the contract. Public Works had shortened the design schedule by 10 months and reduced the proposed consultant contract from $700,000 to $500,000. At that point, construction was expected to begin around August 2020, provided the City approved additional construction funding.8

Even with the shorter design schedule, Wilson called it “almost a four or five year project.” Madison had warned against rushing.8 The years that followed were not a rush.

2021. Selection is deferred

By August 2021, more than three years had passed since the City Council’s April 2018 decision to pursue a vertical barrier. The project had involved design work, public outreach, commission review, and full-scale mockups. The committee now had to decide whether to recommend a design, keep the temporary fence in place for the time being, or take another course.7,9

Option B led among the 1,038 respondents who ranked the mockups. The public survey drew 2,268 responses overall.

Separately, staff reported that 44% of 622 comments preferred no barriers and a return to the bridge’s original state. These were different response groups. Participation was self-selected, so neither result represents Pasadena residents as a whole.9

After reviewing full-scale mockups, the Historic Preservation Commission voted to recommend Option B with modifications. This was the mesh option the City called “curved-curved.” Its barrier curved inward over the walkway along the entire bridge. Option A curved at the lamp posts and alcoves, with straight sections elsewhere. The Design Commission preferred straight mesh panels between the alcoves and requested further study to improve transparency around the alcoves and light poles. When the issue reached the Public Safety Committee, Pasadena Heritage executive director Sue Mossman said the task force itself still hadn’t agreed on a final design.9

Mossman had made the preservation problem plain as early as 2018. Keeping people from the edge was only one goal. The harder task was protecting people while preserving the bridge’s historic character and appearance.23

At the August 18, 2021 Public Safety Committee meeting, Madison said the temporary fencing had been effective and brought up “coupling,” a concept he recalled from Malcolm Gladwell’s 2019 book Talking to Strangers. Gladwell’s account draws on peer-reviewed suicide-prevention studies. The idea is that a suicidal crisis can become tied to a particular place or method. Making access harder can interrupt an attempt without necessarily moving it somewhere else.11,30,32

Researchers use the term “suicide hotspot” for an accessible place where people repeatedly die by suicide and that becomes known for those deaths. A Vancouver study found that some people chose well-known bridges over others closer to home. The researchers suggested that a bridge’s reputation might help explain that pattern.40

On the design question, Madison was not ready to choose. He proposed narrowing the field to two options and taking them through another round of community input.9

Kennedy, who had chaired the committee in 2017, wanted to narrow the options and put the project on a timetable. At the same time, he supported going out “one last time” for additional local expertise to help the City get the decision right.2,9,11

One option still in play had originated with Kennedy. The February 2020 presentation compared straight mesh, pickets, curved mesh, and a hybrid. It projected mockup installation in March, commission reviews in April, and a committee recommendation and Council approval in May. At the meeting, Kennedy requested a cost estimate for enclosing the bridge with a roof before Council consideration.4,10 The City later showed an initial enclosure concept to the task force, including Pasadena Heritage and police and fire representatives. The concept wasn’t accepted because of concerns about appearance and safety.10

At that August meeting, Steve Line of Donald MacDonald Architects presented refined enclosure concepts to the committee. He said one performed especially well when measured against the team’s three design goals of effectiveness, historic compatibility, and appearance.11

Hampton said he had thought Kennedy’s original enclosure idea was a strong one, but wanted something that looked less modern. Madison was more skeptical. He questioned whether preservation advocates or the broader public would accept a barrier that fully enclosed the bridge. Kennedy, who had raised the idea, was now ready to move away from it and narrow the field to the vertical-barrier options.9,11

When Mayor Victor Gordo, who chaired the Public Safety Committee at the time, spoke later in the meeting, he picked up the idea Madison had raised about place and method. He said the temporary structure appeared to be working and that “the decoupling, if you will, has started.” But he still didn’t think the committee was ready to narrow the permanent designs and called for more options and more public input.11

Gordo also took up Kennedy’s enclosure concept. He said he had initially thought full enclosure would never work and had pictured something more like a tunnel. After seeing the refined work, however, he said he liked “the idea of pursuing the material,” wondered whether a different material could be used for the arch-like elements, and said the City shouldn’t rule the concept out.9,11

Hampton also said the temporary structure was working and proposed staffing the bridge 24 hours a day. City Manager Steve Mermell said staffing had been suggested before, would likely cost several hundred thousand dollars a year, and wasn’t feasible as a replacement. He recommended leaving the temporary structure in place until a permanent solution was found.11

Gordo developed the idea using Pasadena’s former Host/Guide program as a model, with trained people carrying radios and serving as additional eyes and ears. He suggested pairing that approach with the City’s ROSE youth-employment program, docents, or tours. Madison pointed to the bridge’s length, curvature, and the speed of some incidents. Kennedy raised liability and intervention concerns. Gordo asked staff to see whether staffing could work alongside a physical barrier.11

“Get it right” became a refrain during the meeting. Mermell, Kennedy, Gordo, and Hampton used the phrase or variations at least nine times, eight within a span of 25 minutes. Near the end, Mermell summarized the requests for more time and a faster result. He then said three months was probably too aggressive and acknowledged that the process ahead would take longer.11

Kennedy also wondered whether the pandemic might help explain why no deaths were recorded at the bridge in 2020 and suggested the question was worth studying. Deaths had already fallen to one in 2019, before the pandemic.3,11

The committee made no selection. The project went back for more design work, community input, and analysis.9,11

John J. Kennedy died on July 21, 2022, while serving on the City Council and only weeks after winning reelection. He had represented District 3 since 2013. Justin Jones was appointed later that year to represent District 3 and would take Kennedy’s place in the bridge discussions that followed. He later won the seat in the March 2024 special election.12

Section 5 of 13 · Open chapter

2022. The first design cycle resets

In September 2022, Public Works returned to the Public Safety Committee with a project reset. The first design cycle, which began with the Donald MacDonald Architects contract in May 2019, had produced three vertical-barrier concepts and the full-scale mockups installed in March 2021.4,8 The committee had declined to recommend one in August 2021.9

Afterward, City staff and Pasadena Heritage sought new design input through the Southern California chapter of the Institute of Classical Architecture & Art. By September 2022, staff was preparing to remove the old mockups, restore the temporary fencing where they had stood, and hire a new design consultant. The report’s schedule anticipated a new consultant by December 2022 or January 2023, design development through June, and a return to the committee around September 2023.4

The next contract didn’t arrive in that window. The first request for proposals drew none, so the City reissued it. Pasadena awarded the new design contract to Apexx Architecture on April 14, 2023, several months after the schedule in the September report.13,24 Pasadena had ended one design cycle and was starting another.

Section 6 of 13 · Open chapter

2023. Previously considered alternatives return

Apexx worked with community and preservation representatives and gathered public input as it developed a new set of barrier concepts. By the November Public Safety Committee meeting, three were under consideration. They were canted webmesh, vertical webmesh, and metal pickets. The webmesh designs used metal mesh stretched between posts, either upright or angled. The picket design used a row of closely spaced vertical metal bars.13,14

Figure 7. Three proposed barrier concepts presented in November 2023. Renderings of proposed designs, not built conditions. Apexx Architecture / City of Pasadena.

At that meeting, the committee returned to questions Pasadena had considered before. Madison, who had moved approval of the task force’s recommendation in 2018, described many suicide attempts as crisis-specific and impulsive and recalled a documentary about people who had survived attempts at the Golden Gate Bridge.6,14

Madison turned to the new designs. He said they would “completely change the aesthetic of the bridge,” while also saying the City had to “elevate human safety over aesthetics and objects.” He added, “But if there’s a way to accomplish both goals, I think we’re going to be more successful.”14

Madison then asked about horizontal netting and landscaping below the bridge. Staff said both had already been studied. Staff explained that the Golden Gate approach did not translate neatly because of the Colorado Street Bridge’s geometry, the remaining fall distance, structural demands, and the appearance of a net below the arches. The 2018 task-force recommendation report said “no verifying scientific data was found” for planting trees below the bridge to “cushion” a landing or discourage an attempt. The same report said trees could not be planted along approximately one-fifth of the span, including its highest point. The task force treated trees as a possible supplement to a vertical barrier, not a replacement.5,14

After hearing those answers, Madison wasn’t ready to support any of the three permanent designs. He suggested the temporary fencing might be preferable to what was being proposed and said it would take a significant change in community sentiment for him to support one of the three concepts.14

Jones was newer to the bridge debate. He hadn’t been part of the Council’s 2018 decision or the committee’s 2021 discussions. At the November 2023 meeting, he relayed concerns from residents who disliked both the temporary fencing and the proposed permanent designs, then asked what other options were available. Staff told him that the alternatives had already been studied extensively and that the Council had previously chosen vertical barriers as the direction.12,14

Jones also asked how involved Public Health had been in the new working group and whether restricting access at one site simply shifted attempts elsewhere. Staff pointed to the mental-health specialist in the process, explained that interrupting access to a selected site generally didn’t send people to the next bridge, and agreed to bring health professionals when the project returned. The Council had considered the same substitution question on April 23, 2018, when Public Health said research showed an overall decline after barriers were installed.7,14

Gordo returned to the staffing idea he had raised in 2021. Deputy Director of Public Works and City Engineer Kris Markarian said the City had already examined staffing and did not consider it cost-effective. Meaningful coverage would require multiple people and a continuous presence because the bridge is long, curved, and cannot be observed from one point. She also cited liability concerns and incidents outside ordinary working hours. Staff considered stationed personnel a possible supplement to a physical barrier, not a replacement.15

Gordo asked for a more specific assessment of trained security at night and Park Safety specialists, docents, or volunteers during the day. Madison questioned whether personnel could respond quickly enough because some incidents can “unfold in five minutes.” Gordo asked staff to consult mental-health professionals about whether a visible human presence could deter an impulsive attempt.15

Near the end of the November 2023 meeting, Public Works Director Tony Olmos and Markarian again explained that a barrier buys time.14

No permanent design was selected. The committee kept the planned commission reviews and public survey moving while asking staff to return with additional work on staffing and other alternatives.14,15

At its December 5, 2023 review, the Historic Preservation Commission recommended vertical webmesh as its first choice and metal pickets as its second.16

Section 7 of 13 · Open chapter

2024. The requested answers come back

When the designs reached the Design Commission in January 2024, its formal assignment was to advise the Council on aesthetic design concepts. The discussion nevertheless moved beyond the appearance of the three designs and returned to whether other safety measures might work.33

Chair Julianna Delgado cited the Golden Gate Bridge and asked whether horizontal netting, landscaping, or another measure could be used instead. An off-camera speaker said horizontal netting had been studied since 2017 and traced the City’s vertical-barrier direction to the task-force review and the Council’s 2018 decision. According to the speaker, a net would create construction, rescue, training, equipment, cost, and architectural problems specific to the Colorado Street Bridge. They said the research the City had reviewed supported a vertical barrier as the most effective option. They also noted that the City’s previous consultant, Donald MacDonald Architects, had served as lead designer of the Golden Gate system and had considered dozens of alternatives for Pasadena before the City retained a new consultant.5,6,33

Delgado then asked whether the public had been shown a horizontal option. The speaker said it had, but that the Council had directed the City to pursue a vertical barrier. Delgado replied that this was “a previous City Council and a long time ago.” One commissioner said the Golden Gate and Colorado Street bridges were structurally different and that a net below the Colorado Street Bridge would compete with its arches. Delgado argued that a net could be placed farther below, suggested planting cacti beneath the bridge, and repeated a public suggestion to construct apartment buildings there. A responding speaker noted that bungalows already existed below the bridge and said the safer approach was to keep people from leaving the bridge deck.33

Later in the meeting, Delgado said she couldn’t support any of the three designs. She said there had to be a better solution and that she didn’t want any of the presented barriers on the bridge.33

The commission did not reach consensus on any of the designs. Its advisory comments called for further study of the metal pickets and canted webmesh, including the angle and details of the canted design, and consideration of combining pickets with webmesh.33

When the project returned to the Public Safety Committee in July 2024, the discussion supplied a more detailed answer to Delgado’s question about horizontal netting. By then, staff’s options had narrowed to vertical webmesh and metal pickets, with canted webmesh listed as eliminated in the July presentation. Staff also returned with much of what elected officials had asked for, including patrol costs, technology analysis, horizontal-netting research, Golden Gate consultation, rescue concerns, survey results, and suicide-prevention expertise.16

Horizontal netting came back with a much fuller engineering and rescue review. The original 2018 task-force comparison had rated it relatively highly for effectiveness but poorly for aesthetics and its impact on emergency services.17 By 2024, after preliminary site-specific engineering work and direct consultation with Golden Gate Bridge officials, Pasadena’s review concluded that horizontal netting wasn’t a viable solution for this bridge.16,18

Fire Chief Chad Augustin explained what a rescue could require. Someone who landed on a net could be 10 or 20 feet below the bridge, making it difficult for police to determine whether the person had a weapon or whether rescuers could safely approach. A rescue could require both of Pasadena’s ladder trucks for hours, along with specialized equipment and additional training. Augustin supported having a barrier on the bridge, but he couldn’t recommend horizontal netting from a public-safety standpoint.18

The committee also received the requested patrol costs. The estimate was roughly $600,000 a year for private security and about $1.16 million a year for law-enforcement coverage. Even at those levels, patrol wasn’t considered an effective substitute for a physical barrier.16

The public survey brought a different kind of answer. It drew 678 responses overall. The City reported that 57% of those answering the need question said a vertical barrier wasn’t needed and 43% said it was. Asked separately whether a vertical barrier would help prevent suicides, 40% said yes, 33% said no, and 27% didn’t know. Some respondents skipped the design ranking, so the overall response count should not be assumed to apply to every question. People chose whether to take the survey, so its results cannot be treated as the views of Pasadena residents as a whole. Staff said 90% of respondents were Pasadena residents, and 74% said they hadn’t participated in the project’s community meetings.19

Among respondents who ranked the designs, metal pickets came first, while the Historic Preservation Commission had preferred vertical webmesh. Lighting drew broader support. The City reported 81% support for added lighting in the survey, and the commission unanimously supported lighting under the balustrade. Those results show support among the participants, not a finding that lighting could replace a barrier.19

Staff saw those results partly as an education problem. Markarian, then acting director of Public Works, said they pointed to a need for “additional education.” Sandri Kramer of Didi Hirsch was even more direct. With roughly 60% of respondents either unconvinced that a vertical barrier would prevent suicides or unsure whether it would, she called it a “public education issue” and explained why restricting access can save lives.18,19

Kramer’s explanation centered on the time between suicidal intent and an attempt. Restricting access, she explained, can allow the immediate danger to ease or create an opening for intervention.19

A separate study of England’s Clifton Suspension Bridge illustrates how that can work. After barriers were installed, bridge staff reported that they had more time to reach people, while deaths fell even though the number of incidents did not. The bridge also used cameras and staff patrols.29

Jones’s questions moved the discussion toward a decision. He asked whether 24/7 patrol was being ruled out because of cost, effectiveness, or both. Staff said both. He then asked whether anyone involved in the expert review had recommended horizontal netting. Staff said no. The recommendation against it had been unanimous. After hearing those answers, Jones said he was prepared to move forward with one of the two remaining designs.18

Hampton pressed for a faster timetable. He asked staff to return in about 90 days and said vertical mesh was his preferred permanent design. He also raised cameras and motion detection as possible supplements. Later in the same discussion, however, after staff said horizontal netting hadn’t been recommended and Fire had explained its rescue concerns, Hampton said he didn’t want the option taken off the table. He still considered it the most attractive of the alternatives.20,21

During that follow-up, Hampton asked about rescue cushions. Fire Chief Chad Augustin said Pasadena’s cushion was rated for roughly 100 feet, while parts of the bridge are about 150 feet high and the equipment couldn’t be positioned everywhere below it. Hampton then asked whether a higher-capacity cushion existed, a question the City hadn’t already answered. Fire did not know and agreed to investigate. A May 2026 Fire Department presentation listed a rescue cushion among its response resources, but gave no capacity rating. That record does not resolve Hampton’s question.21

Eight months after the November discussion of netting and landscaping, Madison again turned to measures below the bridge. He said the City needed an engineering solution and asked about ground cover, physical impediments, and layered netting. Staff and consultants again explained the limits involving fall distance, structure, cost, and what those measures would do to the appearance of the bridge. Madison also repeated his view that human life and safety had to come before objects.18,22

During that exchange, Madison acknowledged that leadership sometimes meant proceeding without a great option. He asked Markarian, “Do you feel like we’ve really done our level best to exhaust all the feasible options?” Markarian described more than seven years on the project and experts with different perspectives. She said, “we have truly exhausted various mitigation measures,” before discussing the Golden Gate comparison.18

Gordo kept technology in the discussion. He asked about cameras and real-time remote intervention, including ways someone might communicate with a person on the bridge during a crisis. Staff continued to treat those tools as possible supplements to a physical barrier, not replacements for one.18

By the end of the meeting, the committee had moved in two directions at once. Jones supported pursuing one of the two remaining vertical designs and developing a plan to bring back to the committee. Hampton had named vertical mesh as his preference. But horizontal netting, below-bridge alternatives, and technology were still in the discussion. No permanent design was sent to the full Council. Staff’s proposed schedule already called for further design work, public feedback, and commission review before a return to the committee in early 2025.18,20,21

Not every delay is the same

Not all of the time this project has taken is attributable to officials revisiting old questions. Work on a protected historic structure had to account for anchoring, maintenance, emergency access, whether the changes could later be undone, and historic character. COVID-19 delayed material procurement and mockup fabrication, and the first request for a replacement design consultant produced no proposals.1,16,24

How to pay for construction remained unresolved. Pasadena had funded the design work, but building the permanent barrier would require separate funding. The Eaton Fire required a major City emergency response in January 2025, though the bridge records reviewed here do not attribute a specific project delay to it.24,25,28

The record also shows real progress. Pasadena chose a physical barrier, funded design work, hired specialists, developed full-scale mockups, and shortened the design schedule by 10 months. Former Pasadena Heritage executive director Sue Mossman later wrote that the process had taken “much longer than anticipated” and that, after so many options and trial runs, “no solution is going to be perfect.”6,8,23

The harder part is what happened after those early decisions. Again and again, staff returned with work meant to narrow the choices. Some questions produced new information. Others brought the committee back to alternatives the City had already studied. A difficult project can take time without every additional round of review being equally necessary.

By now, Pasadena has answers to many of the questions that once made this project uncertain. The Council chose a physical barrier as the main suicide-prevention measure. Staff assessed patrol costs and concluded that stationed personnel could supplement a physical barrier but could not replace one. Horizontal netting ran into engineering and rescue problems specific to this bridge. The 2024 survey also showed substantial doubt among respondents about whether a vertical barrier was needed or would work.6,15,16,18,19

By the later meetings, the differences were increasingly about what officials did after they got an answer. Jones moved toward a choice. Hampton named vertical mesh as his preferred design but asked that horizontal netting stay on the table. Madison continued to press for measures below the bridge. Gordo kept staffing and technology in the discussion.11,14,15,18,20,21

There had been a warning early on about where this could go. In 2018, Mayor Terry Tornek said, “This is probably the highest level of consensus we’ll achieve in this process, I predict.” He said consensus would become much more challenging to achieve once specific designs were rolled out. A few minutes later, as councilmembers debated height and curvature, he called it “a harbinger of the discussion to come.”7

More than eight years have passed since the Council’s April 2018 decision.6 That span reflects, in part, the difficulty of turning agreement on protection into agreement on a design.

Section 12 of 13 · Open chapter

Where that leaves Pasadena

Fewer people died at the bridge in the first few years after the fence went up. Those lower counts were recorded while access remained physically restricted. They do not establish what would happen without the fence.3,28 At the November 2023 committee meeting, a Police Department lieutenant reported, in an oral update, deaths at the bridge in 2022 and 2023, showing that the risk had not disappeared.3

Auckland, New Zealand, offers a documented warning. Grafton Bridge had suicide barriers for decades before the city removed them in 1996 amid concerns about their appearance, effects on the historic structure, and interference with rescue operations. Researchers recorded three deaths from the bridge in 1992–1995, before removal, and 15 in 1997–2000, after removal. After improved curved-glass barriers were installed in 2003, researchers recorded no suicides from the bridge during the following four years, describing the sequence as a natural experiment in what happens when a physical deterrent is present, removed, and restored. Low death counts may show that a barrier is working. They do not mean a bridge has become permanently “decoupled” from the risk of suicide.29

A city shouldn’t need another rise in deaths to remember why access was restricted in the first place.

The bridge’s appearance remains at the center of Pasadena’s debate.14,16

The record increasingly suggests that Pasadena’s unresolved question is how to maintain effective protection and public support for it, whether the City keeps the existing fence or replaces it. Keeping the existing fence in place while Pasadena considers its next step is also a choice. The chain-link fence was installed in response to an emergency. It was not the finished product of the design process that followed.4,28

The funding discussion shows how a temporary arrangement could become a lasting one. In 2025, the City transferred $176,000 from the design-phase account to another bridge project, though staff said enough remained to finish design. In April 2026, further reallocation was discussed, and Public Works warned that it would leave Pasadena without matching funds for a shortlisted application. Construction funding remained unresolved. Without a funded path to a permanent barrier, the temporary fence could become the long-term answer by default.28,31,41

If Pasadena chooses to retain it, the public needs a clear explanation of that choice. Otherwise, the emergency fence risks becoming the public’s picture of what a suicide barrier has to look like. Frustration with its appearance could then become pressure to remove or weaken the protection it provides.28,29

That is where the unresolved design debate becomes a concern about safety. The longer the temporary fence remains, the easier it becomes to see the fence as the problem. Its effect on the bridge is visible every day. The deaths it may prevent are not.3,28
