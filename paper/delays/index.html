Not every delay is the same

Not all of the time this project has taken is attributable to officials revisiting old questions. Work on a protected historic structure had to account for anchoring, maintenance, emergency access, whether the changes could later be undone, and historic character. COVID-19 delayed material procurement and mockup fabrication, and the first request for a replacement design consultant produced no proposals.1,16,24

How to pay for construction remained unresolved. Pasadena had funded the design work, but building the permanent barrier would require separate funding. The Eaton Fire required a major City emergency response in January 2025, though the bridge records reviewed here do not attribute a specific project delay to it.24,25,28

The record also shows real progress. Pasadena chose a physical barrier, funded design work, hired specialists, developed full-scale mockups, and shortened the design schedule by 10 months. Former Pasadena Heritage executive director Sue Mossman later wrote that the process had taken “much longer than anticipated” and that, after so many options and trial runs, “no solution is going to be perfect.”6,8,23

The harder part is what happened after those early decisions. Again and again, staff returned with work meant to narrow the choices. Some questions produced new information. Others brought the committee back to alternatives the City had already studied. A difficult project can take time without every additional round of review being equally necessary.

By now, Pasadena has answers to many of the questions that once made this project uncertain. The Council chose a physical barrier as the main suicide-prevention measure. Staff assessed patrol costs and concluded that stationed personnel could supplement a physical barrier but could not replace one. Horizontal netting ran into engineering and rescue problems specific to this bridge. The 2024 survey also showed substantial doubt among respondents about whether a vertical barrier was needed or would work.6,15,16,18,19

By the later meetings, the differences were increasingly about what officials did after they got an answer. Jones moved toward a choice. Hampton named vertical mesh as his preferred design but asked that horizontal netting stay on the table. Madison continued to press for measures below the bridge. Gordo kept staffing and technology in the discussion.11,14,15,18,20,21

There had been a warning early on about where this could go. In 2018, Mayor Terry Tornek said, “This is probably the highest level of consensus we’ll achieve in this process, I predict.” He said consensus would become much more challenging to achieve once specific designs were rolled out. A few minutes later, as councilmembers debated height and curvature, he called it “a harbinger of the discussion to come.”7

More than eight years have passed since the Council’s April 2018 decision.6 That span reflects, in part, the difficulty of turning agreement on protection into agreement on a design.
